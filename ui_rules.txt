1. DESIGN PHILOSOPHY

Visual: "Municipal Works Record" — light background, correct and unambiguous. The cast-iron hydrant barrel and the leather flap-gate foot valve were assessed, installed, and maintained by municipal waterworks departments operating in full daylight — in the pump station, at the street opening, in the engineering department's drawing office. These are objects of civic infrastructure: they appeared in council specifications, Board of Works tender documents, and municipal engineering reports printed on good paper and filed in town hall archives. The design language draws from that specific documentary tradition: the Victorian municipal engineering report, the waterworks department's installation ledger, the Board of Trade fitting specification. Think the design sensibility of a city engineer's department archive rebuilt as a modern heritage catalog — civic, precise, and carrying the specific authority of public works documentation.

Mood: civic-precise, municipal-authoritative, waterworks-serious, cast-iron infrastructural, public-works weighted.

Important: No fire-fighting nostalgia aesthetics, no vintage fire engine photography used as UI reference, no red-painted hydrant iconography borrowed from modern fire apparatus culture. The mid-19th-century municipal hydrant was a waterworks engineering component specified by the city engineer, not a fire-fighting symbol. The app documents it with the authority of a municipal engineering office, not a fire heritage museum. The cast iron and the leather belong to the waterworks tradition, and the UI should honor that tradition specifically.

3. TYPOGRAPHY

Display / Headings: Archivo (weight 700) — returns from Whistles in the Mist, Echoes of the Survey Line, Shuttles of the Mill Row, Gauze of the Underworld, and Glow in the Damp, where it carried infrastructure, regulatory, and civil engineering authority. Here it carries municipal engineering authority — the same wide uppercase geometry that read like a railway safety approval or a bridge survey header now reads like a Board of Works hydrant specification, a borough engineer's tender document, or a municipal waterworks installation standard. The typography of Victorian civic infrastructure is consistent across railway, bridge, and waterworks engineering, and Archivo honors that continuity.
Body / Descriptions: IBM Plex Sans (weight 300–400) — engineered and institutional. For condition notes, municipal district history, frost-case sleeve notes, and all prose fields.
Monospace / Identifiers: IBM Plex Mono — for archive codes like DHV-STANTON-CASTI-HYDRANT-1871-GBR-BIRMINGHAM-BWKS-033, barrel bore (Ø: 76mm), operating nut drive ratio (1:12 square drive), head loss coefficient (K: 4.2), and all hydraulic head-loss simulator input and output parameters.

Important: Archivo in uppercase for hydrant type designations and municipal specification headers — a Board of Works hydrant standard was always cited in uppercase in the engineering record, with the manufacturer's name and bore size as the primary identifying fields. IBM Plex Mono for every bore dimension, drive ratio, and hydraulic parameter — waterworks fitting specifications were held to British Standard tolerances from the 1860s onward, and the typography must carry that standardization authority.

HYDRAULIC HEAD-LOSS SIMULATOR — DESIGN SPECIFICATION

The hydraulic head-loss simulator is the defining technical feature of the app and the tenth simulation environment in the series. It models a ninth category of physical behavior — pressure head loss through historical cast-iron fittings and leather valve assemblies — the second fluid dynamics simulation in the series after the Great Breath's pneumatic flow analyzer, but modeling an incompressible liquid system at municipal supply pressures rather than the compressible air dynamics of an organ windchest.

Input environment: light panel (
#EDF0EC) within the overall council paper white — following the established convention for civil engineering simulators in the series. The hydraulic head-loss calculation was performed in the waterworks engineer's office in full daylight, using published loss coefficient tables and hydraulic gradient calculations. The simulation panel continues the light environment of the archival register, with a slightly cooler and greener tint referencing the specific quality of a waterworks department's technical drawing paper.
Input fields in IBM Plex Mono: supply pressure (kPa or psi or metres head), barrel bore diameter (mm), barrel length (mm), foot valve type (from material database: leather flap-gate, ball, disc), leather condition (new, serviceable, worn, cracked), frost case sleeve clearance (mm), operating nut drive ratio (1:8, 1:10, 1:12, 1:16), pipe roughness (cast iron new, cast iron tuberculated, wrought iron), and flow rate demand (L/min or GPM).
Hydraulic gradient diagram: SVG rendered on the light panel — a side elevation of the hydrant assembly showing the hydraulic grade line as a sloped line above the physical fitting, dropping across each resistance element: the foot valve shown as a step drop in the grade line, the barrel friction as a gradual slope, the frost case restriction as a small additional step, the operating nut constriction as a final step at the outlet. Each drop labeled with its component head loss in IBM Plex Mono. The total available head at the hydrant outlet shown as the final grade line elevation above the outlet datum — the number a fire brigade engineer needed to know before connecting their hose.
Head-loss outputs in IBM Plex Mono: foot valve loss (ΔH_valve: 1.2m), barrel friction loss (ΔH_friction: 0.4m), frost sleeve loss (ΔH_sleeve: 0.3m), total fitting loss (ΔH_total: 1.9m), available outlet pressure (P_outlet: 182 kPa), flow velocity (v: 2.4 m/s), Reynolds number (Re: 182,400), flow regime (TURBULENT / TRANSITIONAL), and a municipal adequacy verdict (ADEQUATE FOR FIRE SERVICE / MARGINAL / INSUFFICIENT) based on the minimum supply pressure standards of the originating municipal waterworks authority.
Leather valve degradation model: a secondary panel unique to this simulator — modeling the progressive increase in head loss as the leather flap-gate deteriorates from new through serviceable to cracked condition. At each condition state, the foot valve loss coefficient increases and the available outlet pressure decreases. The model outputs the minimum leather condition at which the hydrant meets the municipal adequacy standard — the threshold below which the maintenance crew should have replaced the leather. This is the most maintenance-historical output in any simulation in the series: it connects a physical condition assessment to a documented maintenance obligation.
Historical pressure survey comparison: a tertiary section where the computed outlet pressure is displayed alongside any surviving municipal pressure survey data for the district — Victorian waterworks departments conducted regular pressure surveys at hydrant locations, and where these records survive, the simulator's computed values can be compared against the historical measured pressures. Convergence validates the hydrant's specification record. Divergence flags either a condition change in the fitting or an error in the historical survey — both significant findings for a municipal engineering historian.

Important: The hydraulic head-loss simulator is the most publicly consequential simulation in the series — a hydrant that cannot deliver adequate pressure at the outlet is a hydrant that cannot supply a fire hose at the moment of a building fire. The municipal adequacy verdict is the single most operationally significant output in the simulator, and it should be rendered with the visual weight appropriate to a public safety judgment: waterworks green for adequate, leather valve brown for marginal, critical errors red for insufficient. The leather valve degradation model is the most maintenance-historical feature in any simulation — it quantifies the engineering consequence of a maintenance decision that was made or deferred by a waterworks foreman on a specific date, and it connects the physical object in the collection to the human decision-making chain that determined whether a Victorian city was protected from fire. That connection — between the object in the ledger and the life-safety consequence it represented — is the most important thing this app communicates, and the simulator should make it impossible to miss.
